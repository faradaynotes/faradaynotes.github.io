What’s Actually Inside Donut Lab’s Solid-State Battery?

Donut Lab claims a solid-state battery delivering 400 Wh/kg, 5-minute charging, and 100,000-cycle life: all without lithium, nickel, or cobalt. These numbers come from Donut Lab's public communications and have not been independently verified in the VTT report analyzed here. The extracted raw data from that report tell a more complicated story. By reconstructing the voltage–capacity profiles and differential-capacity fingerprints, we can ask a simpler question: what kind of electrochemistry would have to exist to produce these curves?

Donut Lab’s public claims are not modest. The company points to high energy density, rapid charging, operation across extreme temperatures, improved intrinsic safety, and most strikingly, a solid-state chemistry reportedly free of lithium, nickel, and cobalt. Any one of those claims would deserve careful scrutiny.

In this post, we analyze the baseline cycling data from Figure 2 of Donut Lab Solid-State Battery V1 Charge Performance Test [1]: the 1C reference cycles that form the electrochemical foundation of everything that follows. We extracted the raw data from the published figure and analyzed it at three levels: the voltage–capacity profile, the derived energy and coulombic efficiency metrics (capacity loss during formation cycles), and the incremental capacity signature (dQ/dV versus voltage), which serves as a chemical fingerprint of the redox processes active inside the cell.

This article focuses only on Figure 2 of the VTT report: the initial capacity test and reference-style 1C cycling. The fast-charge sections (Figures 3 and 4) deserve their own treatment because they provide a different set of information.

One claim above all others

For the purposes of this post, only one claim matters most: Donut Lab presents the battery as a solid-state chemistry that excludes Li, Ni, and Co. If that is true, the baseline electrochemical fingerprint should look meaningfully different from familiar layered-oxide systems.

  • A nominal 26 Ah pouch cell tested over a 2.7–4.15 V recommended voltage window.
  • The VTT protocol used a 24 A CC–CV charge to 4.15 V (cutoff 1.2 A), followed by 24 A discharges to 2.7 V, with 1-hour rest periods between steps.
  • Two visible charge events and two visible discharge events in Figure 2 of the VTT report, with a formation-like first cycle and a stabilized second cycle.

What the voltage–capacity curve reveals

The reconstructed voltage–capacity curve reduces the test to its electrochemical essentials. Figure 1 shows the first and second cycle profiles. The first cycle exhibits a charge capacity of ~28.7 Ah followed by a discharge of only ~26.10 Ah, a loss of ~2.6 Ah corresponding to a coulombic efficiency of ~90.9%. This irreversible capacity loss reflects the one-time processes that occur during initial cell charging, broadly associated with formation: interfacial layer growth, structural reorganization, and other reactions that consume capacity irreversibly. Manufacturers typically run these formation cycles before shipping. In the second cycle, coulombic efficiency recovers to ~99.7%, consistent with a stabilized system (see Table 2).

Figure 1. Reconstructed voltage–capacity curves from Figure 2 of the VTT report. The first cycle (magenta) includes a larger initial charge and visible formation-related loss; the second cycle (gold) is more stable and returns to the nominal ~26 Ah discharge behavior. The smooth, sloping profiles and high-voltage upper shoulder are the first clues that this is not a simple plateau-dominated chemistry.

The operating voltage window is high, pointing to a cathode in the high-voltage intercalation family (NMC, NCA, LMO, LNMO, and related systems) rather than a low-voltage insertion or conversion chemistry. The voltage profile shows no single dominant plateau, ruling out typical phosphate-like behavior. The charge–discharge voltage gap of ~0.35–0.37 V is modest for a solid-state cell at 1C, suggesting reasonably fast interfacial kinetics (see Table 2).

A 4.15 V upper cutoff and a ~3.48–3.49 V average discharge voltage place this cell firmly in the domain of high-voltage intercalation systems. This voltage window is also consistent with certain sodium-ion layered oxide chemistries — P2-type and O3-type NaxMO2 systems operate across a comparable range — a possibility we return to in the fingerprint analysis below.

What the VTT report measures — and what its table structure leaves implicit

Figure 1 shows the full sequence, beginning with the initial charge and then the first discharge. In the VTT report, Cycle 1 is defined starting from the first discharge. As a result, the charge capacity associated with that initial charge is absent from VTT Table 3 (shown here as Table 1) and the entry appears as “—”. Notably, the report does not comment on this absent charge event or quantify the capacity loss that occurred during it — which corresponds to the ~2.6 Ah difference between the first charge and first discharge visible in Figure 1.

This means the most informative number in the entire initial test — the irreversible first-cycle loss — is not visible in the reported table at all.

The chemical fingerprint: incremental capacity analysis

The voltage–capacity curve can be processed further to extract more information about the underlying cathode and/or anode. For that, we turn to incremental capacity analysis (dQ/dV), which amplifies subtle features in the voltage profile. Each electrochemical system produces a characteristic pattern of peaks and features that act as a fingerprint of the electrode materials and their phase transitions.

Figure 2. Incremental capacity (dQ/dV) curves of two cycles from Donut Lab’s solid-state cell cycled at 1C, derived from the voltage–capacity data in Figure 1.

Figure 2 shows the dQ/dV curves of two cycles from Donut Lab’s solid-state cell cycled at 1C, derived from the voltage–capacity data (Figure 1). The fingerprint shows multiple distinct peaks across the voltage range, with the main charge-side features appearing around ~3.52 V, ~3.68 V, and ~3.97 V, and corresponding features on the discharge curve (Figure 2). This multi-peak structure in a high-voltage window is the defining feature of the electrochemical response.

To interpret this, the extracted fingerprint can be compared with reference incremental-capacity curves from well-established industrial Li-ion chemistries. Figure 3 overlays the Donut Lab curves with reference profiles to assess whether the observed structure aligns with any known system. The comparison does not yield a clean match. The overall shape resembles high-voltage intercalation behavior, but the peak positions, relative intensities, and spacing do not align consistently with standard systems such as NMC, NCA, LMO, or LCO (Figure 3).

At the same time, this comparison supports the company’s claim in a narrow sense: the fingerprint does not correspond cleanly to any standard Li-ion battery chemistry. However, the electrochemical behavior still sits firmly within the high-voltage intercalation regime. A cell operating over a 2.7–4.15 V window, with ~3.48–3.49 V average discharge voltage and multiple high-voltage dQ/dV peaks (Figure 2), must be governed by oxide-like redox behavior—even if the exact composition is different.

Conclusion: Electrochemical fingerprint narrows the field more than the marketing does

Figure 2 does not identify the exact chemistry, but it constrains it tightly. It shows a high-voltage, intercalation-like system with strong first-cycle irreversibility, stable ~26 Ah discharge behavior in the second cycle after formation, and a differential-capacity fingerprint that resembles lithium (or sodium) transition metal oxides without matching any standard reference cleanly.

Very few cathode–solid electrolyte combinations can produce this behavior in a solid-state cell at these fast charging times (~5 minutes). These are not among today’s industrialized Li-ion chemistries, and they are not the heavily marketed materials either. They are also not a mystery, but rather systems that exist in the battery literature, often overlooked or buried in less prominent work. In the next post, we break down the specific chemistries that can realistically deliver this combination of high voltage and fast kinetics in solid-state batteries.

Methodology: how the data were extracted and validated

The analysis above depends on reconstructed data, so the extraction method has to be explicit. Voltage–time and current–time curves were digitized from the published Figure 2 traces in the VTT customer report at high resolution. The two signals were then time-aligned before calculating capacity and energy, ensuring that the CC–CV transition and the subsequent current decay were represented consistently rather than inferred loosely.

To validate the reconstruction, the extracted traces were overlaid directly on the original plotted curves. In the validation plots below, the extracted traces are shown in red and the original curves in blue. Across the full cycle sequence—including the CC regions, the CV decay, and the discharge segments—the overlap is visually tight.

Figure 4. Validation of the digitized Figure 2 data. Original curves are shown in blue and reconstructed curves in red. The extracted traces reproduce the published voltage and current profiles closely across both cycles, including the CC regions, the CV transition, and the current taper. These reconstructed traces are the basis for the analysis in Figures 1 and 2.

Key derived quantities—capacity, energy, and average voltage—fall within approximately 0.12% of the values reported in VTT Table 3. That level of agreement is sufficient for the purpose of electrochemical interpretation, especially because the main conclusions in this article rely on robust structural features of the curves: the operating voltage window, the first-cycle irreversibility, the existence of multiple differential-capacity peaks, and the mismatch with standard reference chemistries. Those are not artifacts of extraction. They are properties of the published data itself.
